Lab P5 · Mixtures: partial molar properties and component fugacity

What does a component contribute to a mixture?

Two measured data sets. Densities of ethanol + water at 298.15 K give the excess volume and, by the tangent-intercept rule, the partial molar volume of each component. Measured cross second virial coefficients of three gas pairs at 298.15 K give the fugacity coefficient of each component in a gas mixture, against combining rules, Peng–Robinson and the Lewis–Randall rule.

Data and method

VE = V − x₁V₁* − x₂V₂* from the measured densities and the pure densities used by the authors; it reproduces their reported VE to 0.001 cm³/mol. The fit is linear least squares, unweighted. Pure Bii at 298.15 K: methane −42.78, CO₂ −122.89, ethane −184.96 cm³/mol (NIST isotherms, gas points up to 0.6 mol/L). The virial equation is truncated after B, so use it up to about half the dew pressure; the lab allows up to 60 bar for comparison with Peng–Robinson.

    A. Why is a mole of ethanol in water smaller than a mole of pure ethanol?

    Compare V̄ of ethanol at infinite dilution with its pure molar volume, 58.68 cm³/mol.

    Compare with an explanation

    With three Redlich–Kister terms, V̄ of ethanol at infinite dilution is about 52 cm³/mol, 6–7 cm³/mol less than pure ethanol. VE is negative across the whole range, with a minimum of −1.1 cm³/mol near x = 0.38: water and ethanol pack more tightly together than apart, through hydrogen bonding and the hydration of the ethyl group. A partial molar volume is a derivative of the mixture volume, not the size of a molecule.

    B. Why do V̄₁ and V̄₂ move in opposite directions?

    Use the partial-molar-volume view and read the slopes at x = 0.3.

    Compare with an explanation

    Gibbs–Duhem at fixed T and P: x₁dV̄₁ + x₂dV̄₂ = 0. If one partial molar volume rises with x₁, the other must fall, weighted by the mole fractions. The lab's check gives about 10⁻⁹ cm³/mol for any fit, because partial molar volumes from one V(x) satisfy it by construction. Measured activity coefficients do not, which is why Session 2 after the midterm uses Gibbs–Duhem to test data.

    C. When does the Lewis–Randall rule fail?

    CO₂ + methane at 30 bar: compare φ̂ of CO₂ from the measured B₁₂ with the Lewis–Randall value at y₁ = 0.05 and 0.95.

    Compare with an explanation

    Lewis–Randall sets δ₁₂ = 0. The measured B₁₂ = −64.8 cm³/mol gives δ₁₂ = +36.1 cm³/mol: CO₂ and methane attract each other less than the average of the pure pairs. At y₁ = 0.05 the measured φ̂ of CO₂ is 0.896 against 0.862 from Lewis–Randall (4% apart). At y₁ = 0.95 the y₂² factor is 0.0025 and the two agree. The rule fails for a dilute component whose unlike interactions differ from the like ones.

    What these results can and cannot show

    • Nine density points cover water mole fractions from 0.10 to 0.91. Below a mole fraction of about 0.09 of either component nothing is measured, so infinite-dilution values are extrapolations of the fitted form.
    • The cross virial coefficients are single measurements with stated uncertainty, at one temperature. A k₁₂ fitted to one B₁₂ is not validated at other T or P.
    • The truncated virial equation is a low-density model. Peng–Robinson at 60 bar is an extrapolation of the same information plus k₁₂.
