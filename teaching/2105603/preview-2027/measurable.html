Lab P1 · Measurable properties from PVT data and Cp

What can PVT data and Cp tell you?

Six states of four fluids, tabulated from the NIST reference equations of state. The lab differentiates V(T, P) and S(T, P) along an isobar and an isotherm to obtain α, κT, Cp − Cv, the Joule–Thomson coefficient μJT and the internal pressure (∂U/∂V)T, and compares them with the tabulated Cv and μJT.

Compute the properties by finite differences, compare them with the ideal gas and the reference values, and decide what the state of the fluid and the step size do to the result.

Data and method

Each state carries 201 rows of an isobar (T ± 20 K in steps of 0.2 K) and an isotherm around P. Derivatives use the rows δ away from the state. The tables carry twelve significant digits. With fewer digits, a very small step loses accuracy to cancellation: try it in your own code. 1 L bar = 100 J converts the PV terms to J.

    4. Check your understanding

    Write first, then compare

    Use Steps 1–3 to answer each question in your own words. Then open the explanation and compare. Your answers are saved with your study.

    A. Why is Cp − Cv of methane at 100 bar more than twice R?

    Calculate the dense methane state and compare α, κT and V with the ideal gas.

    Compare with the explanation

    At 300 K and 100 bar, α = 5.79 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹ against 1/T = 3.33 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹ for the ideal gas (1.74 times), κT = 0.0112 bar⁻¹ against 1/P = 0.0100 bar⁻¹ (1.12 times), and Z = 0.856. Cp − Cv scales as Zα²/κT: 0.856 × 1.74²/1.12 = 2.31, so 19.2 J/(mol K) instead of R = 8.31.

    The dense gas expands more on heating than an ideal gas because attraction is weakened as the molecules separate. That extra expansion work is what Cp pays for and Cv does not.

    B. Why does liquid water warm up when it is throttled?

    Calculate liquid water and read the sign of μJT and of Tα − 1.

    Compare with the explanation

    For water at 298.15 K, Tα = 298.15 × 2.57 × 10⁻⁴ = 0.077, far below 1. With μJT = V(Tα − 1)/Cp = 0.0181 L/mol × (−0.923) × 100/(75.3 J/(mol K)) = −0.0221 K/bar, a drop of 100 bar warms the water by about 2.2 K.

    A gas cools on throttling only where Tα > 1, that is, where it expands more on heating than the ideal gas would at the same T. A liquid barely expands, so the flow work dominates and it warms.

    C. Why does a forward difference fail near the critical point?

    Calculate CO₂ at 310 K and 80 bar with δT = 1 K, first central and then forward. Repeat with δT = 0.2 K.

    Compare with the explanation

    With δT = 1 K the central difference gives μJT = 0.763 K/bar (+4.0%) and the forward difference 0.644 K/bar (−12.2%). With δT = 0.2 K the central result is within 0.2%. For dense methane, δT = 1 K is already within 0.01%.

    The forward error is proportional to δT × V″, the central error to δT² × V‴. Near the critical point V(T) bends sharply, so both grow, the forward one fastest. A step that is safe in one state is not safe in another.

    4. Investigate what the tables can support

    Graduate work

    Calculate a state in Step 1 before running an investigation.

    What these results can and cannot show

    • The tables come from one fundamental equation per fluid, so Maxwell relations hold by construction. Agreement here tests your arithmetic, not the data. Measurements from different sources would not agree this well.
    • The reference μJT and Cv come from the same equation of state. The comparison measures finite-difference error, not the accuracy of the equation of state.
    • Throttling is treated as adiabatic and steady with negligible change in kinetic energy: Hin = Hout.
