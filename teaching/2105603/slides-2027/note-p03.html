Residual properties: the real-fluid part of H, S and G

Pre-class note for Session P3 · about 20 minutes

Soorathep Kheawhom, Ph.D., FRSC · 2105603 Advanced Chemical Engineering Thermodynamics

Draft for 2027. If you are taking the course this year, use the current course page.

Why read this. Session P2 compared equations of state on Z. A compressor, a valve or a heat exchanger needs H and S, and phase equilibrium needs G. This note shows how all three follow from an equation of state through the residual properties, and why an equation that predicts Z well can still predict H poorly. Work the three questions at the end and bring one question of your own.

1. Definition and the ideal-gas path

A residual property is the difference between the real fluid and an ideal gas at the same T and P:

M^R(T,P)=M(T,P)-M^{\text{ig}}(T,P),\qquad M=V,\,U,\,H,\,S,\,A,\,G.

Because H and S are state functions, any change between two states can follow a path through the ideal gas: convert the real fluid at state 1 into an ideal gas (−M₁R), change the ideal gas from state 1 to state 2, and convert back (+M₂R):

\Delta H=H_2^R-H_1^R+\int_{T_1}^{T_2}C_p^{\text{ig}}\,dT,\qquad \Delta S=S_2^R-S_1^R+\int_{T_1}^{T_2}\frac{C_p^{\text{ig}}}{T}\,dT-R\ln\frac{P_2}{P_1}.

The ideal-gas terms need only Cpig(T), which comes from spectroscopy or tables. The residual terms need only PVT data or an equation of state. This is the plan announced in Session P1.

2. Residuals from PVT data

At constant T, (∂G/∂P)T = V for both the real fluid and the ideal gas, so

G^R=\int_0^P\Big(V-\frac{RT}{P}\Big)dP\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{G^R}{RT}=\int_0^P(Z-1)\frac{dP}{P}.

The lower limit is P = 0, where every fluid is ideal and all residuals vanish. Differentiating GR/RT with respect to T (the Gibbs–Helmholtz relation, H = −T²[∂(G/T)/∂T]P) gives

\frac{H^R}{RT}=-T\int_0^P\Big(\frac{\partial Z}{\partial T}\Big)_P\frac{dP}{P},\qquad \frac{S^R}{R}=\frac{H^R}{RT}-\frac{G^R}{RT}.

The key point: GR needs Z along one isotherm; HR and SR also need how Z changes with T. For pressure-explicit equations such as the cubics the same results are written as integrals over density.

3. The virial equation

With Z = 1 + BP/RT the integrals are exact:

\frac{G^R}{RT}=\frac{BP}{RT},\qquad \frac{H^R}{RT}=\frac{P}{R}\Big(\frac{B}{T}-\frac{dB}{dT}\Big),\qquad \frac{S^R}{R}=-\frac{P}{R}\frac{dB}{dT}.

With the Pitzer correlation, d(BPc/RTc)/dTr = 0.675/Tr2.6 + ω × 0.722/Tr5.2.

Worked example. CO₂ at 300 K and 10 bar: Tr = 0.9864, B = −122.7 cm³/mol and dB/dT = 0.985 cm³/(mol K). Then GR = BP = −122.7 J/mol, HR = P(B − T dB/dT) = 10⁶ Pa × (−122.7 − 295.4) × 10⁻⁶ m³/mol = −418.2 J/mol, and SR = −0.985 J/(mol K). The NIST reference gives −418.3 J/mol and −0.983 J/(mol K). At 10 bar the gas is dilute enough for the truncated form.

4. Cubic equations of state

For the generic cubic of Session P2, with β = bP/RT, q = a(T)/(bRT) and I = ln[(Z + σβ)/(Z + εβ)]/(σ − ε):

\frac{G^R}{RT}=Z-1-\ln(Z-\beta)-qI,\qquad \frac{H^R}{RT}=Z-1+\Big[\frac{d\ln\alpha}{d\ln T_r}-1\Big]qI,\qquad \frac{S^R}{R}=\ln(Z-\beta)+\frac{d\ln\alpha}{d\ln T_r}\,qI.

For van der Waals I = β/Z and d ln α/d ln Tr = 0. For SRK and PR, d ln α/d ln Tr = −m√Tr/[1 + m(1 − √Tr)]. Use the same root Z for all three. m(ω) was fitted to vapor pressures, never to enthalpies, so HR and SR test the equation in a way that Z and Psat do not.

5. The ideal-gas part

NIST gives Cpig in the Shomate form, with t = T/1000:

C_p^{\text{ig}}=A+Bt+Ct^2+Dt^3+E/t^2,

and its integrals for H and S° at 1 bar. At pressure P, Sig(T, P) = S°(T) − R ln(P/1 bar). For CO₂ between 300 and 350 K the ideal-gas changes are ΔHig = 1917 J/mol and ΔS°ig = 5.91 J/(mol K). Each Shomate fit has a stated temperature range; for methane and CO₂ it starts at 298 K.

6. Before Session P3

Write short answers. We use them in the first 30 minutes.

  1. Signs. For CO₂ at 300 K and 10 bar, HR < 0 and SR < 0. Explain both in terms of molecular attraction. Then say why GR = HR − TSR is much smaller in magnitude than HR.
  2. A valve. A gas passes a valve with no heat or work. Write the enthalpy balance with residuals and show that an ideal gas leaves at its inlet temperature.
  3. Where the error enters. Two cubic equations give the same Z at 300 K and 100 bar but different α(T) slopes. Which of GR, HR and SR will differ between them?
