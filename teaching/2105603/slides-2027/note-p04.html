Fugacity and the equilibrium of a pure fluid

Pre-class note for Session P4 · about 20 minutes

Soorathep Kheawhom, Ph.D., FRSC · 2105603 Advanced Chemical Engineering Thermodynamics

Draft for 2027. If you are taking the course this year, use the current course page.

Why read this. Session P1 showed that two phases in equilibrium have equal chemical potentials. The chemical potential has no absolute value and tends to −∞ as P → 0, which makes it awkward to compute with. The fugacity replaces it with a quantity in pressure units that behaves well. This note defines it, shows how to compute it from the residual Gibbs energy of Session P3, and uses it for the vapor pressure, the Clapeyron equation and the fugacity of liquids and solids. Work the three questions at the end and bring one question of your own.

1. Definition

For an ideal gas at fixed T, dμ = V dP = RT d ln P. For any fluid, the fugacity f is defined so that the same form holds:

d\mu=RT\,d\ln f\quad(T\ \text{fixed}),\qquad \lim_{P\to0}\frac{f}{P}=1.

The fugacity coefficient is φ = f/P. Integrating dμ − dμig from P = 0 at fixed T gives, for a pure fluid,

\ln\varphi=\frac{G^R}{RT}=\int_0^P(Z-1)\frac{dP}{P}.

So ln φ is the GR/RT of Session P3. From the truncated virial equation ln φ = BP/RT; from a cubic, ln φ = Z − 1 − ln(Z − β) − qI for the chosen root.

Worked example. CO₂ vapor at 280 K and 30 bar. The NIST reference gives Z = 0.779 and φ = 0.819, so f = 24.6 bar. With the Pitzer B = −144.7 cm³/mol, ln φ = −0.186 and f = 24.9 bar. For phase equilibrium the gas behaves as an ideal gas at 24.6 bar, not 30.

2. Equal fugacity and the vapor pressure

Two phases of a pure fluid at the same T and P are in equilibrium when μL = μV, that is

f^L=f^V\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad\ln\varphi^L=\ln\varphi^V.

Below Tc a cubic has two outer roots between its two spinodal pressures (Session P2). Its vapor pressure is the pressure in that window where the two roots have equal ln φ. A practical algorithm:

  1. find the spinodal pressures Pmin and Pmax from (∂P/∂V)T = 0, using max(Pmin, a small positive pressure) as the lower bound;
  2. at each trial P, take the smallest and largest real roots with Z > β and compute ln φL − ln φV;
  3. find the zero by bisection or the secant method.

Outside the window there is only one root, and ln φL = ln φV is satisfied trivially by that single root. A solver started outside the window can return this meaningless answer.

For propane at 300 K, Peng–Robinson gives Psat = 9.975 bar and φsat = 0.843; NIST gives 9.977 bar and 0.846.

3. The Clapeyron equation

Moving along the coexistence curve, dμL = dμV. With dμ = −S dT + V dP for each phase and ΔS = ΔH/T at equilibrium,

\frac{dP^{sat}}{dT}=\frac{\Delta H_{vap}}{T\,\Delta V}.

This is exact. With VL neglected and VV = RT/P it becomes the Clausius–Clapeyron equation, d ln Psat/dT = ΔHvap/RT². For propane at 300 K, NIST gives ΔHvap = 14.66 kJ/mol, VL = 90.1 and VV = 2039 cm³/mol, so dPsat/dT = 0.251 bar/K. Near Tc, ΔV → 0 and the vapor is far from ideal; Clausius–Clapeyron then fails badly.

4. Fugacity of a compressed liquid

From (∂ ln f/∂P)T = V/RT, integrated from saturation where fL = fV = φsatPsat:

f^L(P)=\varphi^{sat}P^{sat}\exp\int_{P^{sat}}^{P}\frac{V^L}{RT}\,dP\approx\varphi^{sat}P^{sat}\exp\frac{V^L(P-P^{sat})}{RT}.

The exponential is the Poynting factor. Because VL is small it stays close to 1 unless P − Psat is tens of bar. This is the liquid-side expression used in the γ–φ form of VLE in Session 1 after the midterm.

5. Fugacity of a solid

Below its melting temperature Tm, a substance’s stable condensed phase is the solid. Its fugacity is written relative to the subcooled liquid at the same T, a metastable state that serves as a reference. Integrating the Gibbs–Helmholtz relation between T and Tm and neglecting ΔCp,

\ln\frac{f_s}{f_l}=-\frac{\Delta H_{fus}}{R}\Big(\frac{1}{T}-\frac{1}{T_m}\Big).

The ratio is less than 1 below Tm, which is why the solid is stable there. Session 4 after the midterm uses this for solid solubility and freezing points.

6. Before Session P4

Write short answers. We use them in the first 30 minutes.

  1. Vapor fugacity. For CO₂ at 280 K and 10 bar, compute φ and f from the truncated virial equation with B = −144.7 cm³/mol. Why is the correction smaller than at 30 bar?
  2. Clapeyron. From the propane numbers in Section 3, estimate Psat at 302 K. Then compute ΔHvap from Clausius–Clapeyron with the same slope, and say which assumption causes the difference.
  3. Poynting. Liquid water at 373.15 K has Psat = 1.014 bar and VL = 18.80 cm³/mol. Compute the Poynting factor at 2, 10 and 100 bar. At which pressure would you stop setting it to 1?
