Solids in liquids, and gases on surfaces

Pre-class note for Session 4 · about 20 minutes

Soorathep Kheawhom · 2105603 Advanced Chemical Engineering Thermodynamics

Draft for 2027. If you are taking the course this year, use the current course page.

Why read this. Sessions 1–3 dealt with fluid phases. Session 4 adds a phase whose composition we usually know in advance: a pure solid, either crystallizing from a liquid or holding gas on its surface. The equilibrium condition is the same, equal chemical potential, but the solid supplies a fixed reference. Work the three questions at the end and bring one question of your own.

1. A pure solid in equilibrium with a liquid

When component 1 crystallizes as a pure solid (no solid solution), equilibrium requires f_1^{\text{s}}=f_1^{\text{l}}, with f_1^{\text{l}}=x_1\gamma_1f_1^{\text{l,pure}}. The ratio of the pure-solid to the pure (subcooled) liquid fugacity follows from a path through the melting point:

\ln(x_1\gamma_1)=-\frac{\Delta_{\text{fus}}H}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T}-\frac{1}{T_m}\right)+\frac{\Delta C_p}{R}\left(\frac{T_m}{T}-1-\ln\frac{T_m}{T}\right)

The ΔCp term is small when T is near Tm and is usually neglected; we do so in Session 4. The equation assumes a pure solid of one crystal form. A solid solution or a polymorph changes the left side.

With γ₁ = 1 the result is the ideal solubility. It depends only on the solute’s fusion properties, not on the solvent. Every solvent that differs from it does so through γ₁.

2. Fusion data decide the ideal solubility

For naphthalene, two sources give:

Source Tm / K ΔfusH / (kJ mol⁻¹) ideal x₁ at 298.15 K
NIST WebBook: triple point (average of 6); ΔfusH of Chirico et al. (2002) 353.39 19.0 0.302
DSC in Xu et al. (2019), ±2.12 K and ±2.05 kJ/mol 355.04 20.87 0.260

At 298.15 K, each 1 kJ/mol in ΔfusH changes ln x₁ by 0.063. The two sets differ by 16 % in ideal solubility. Any γ₁ inferred from a measured solubility inherits that difference.

3. From measured solubility to γ₁

Xu et al. report the saturation temperature at a given molality of naphthalene, m in mol per kg of solvent. Convert first:

x_1=\frac{m}{m+1000/M_{\text{solvent}}}

Then γ₁ = x₁id(T)/x₁ at the row’s own temperature.

Worked example. In octane (M = 114.229 g/mol), m = 1.3217 mol/kg saturates at 296.6 K. So x₁ = 1.3217/(1.3217 + 8.7544) = 0.1312. With the NIST fusion data, x₁id(296.6 K) = 0.290, and γ₁ = 2.21. Octane dissolves naphthalene less than half as well as an ideal solvent would.

A one-parameter model, ln γ₁ = (W/RT) x₂², then gives an equation in which x₁ appears on both sides. It has to be solved for x₁; it cannot be evaluated once.

4. Two solids: the eutectic

When both components can crystallize, each has its own liquidus curve from Section 1. They meet at the eutectic, where liquid coexists with both solids. For a binary at fixed P the phase rule gives F = 2 − 3 + 1 = 0: the eutectic temperature and composition are fixed. Lab 10 builds this diagram and follows a feed as it cools.

5. A gas on a solid surface

Adsorption is also an equality of chemical potential, but between the gas and an adsorbed phase. Its result is an isotherm, the amount adsorbed q (mol/kg) as a function of P at fixed T. Two common forms:

\text{Henry: } q=KP\qquad\text{Langmuir: } q=\frac{q_sbP}{1+bP}

Henry’s law is the low-pressure limit of Langmuir, with K = qsb. In a closed vessel of volume V with m kg of adsorbent and n mol of gas, the pressure satisfies

n=\frac{PV}{RT}+m\,q(P,T).

Example (Lab 11). V = 1 L, m = 20 g, n = 0.03 mol, 300 K, Langmuir qs = 2 mol/kg, b = 0.1 kPa⁻¹: P = 15.0 kPa, q = 1.20 mol/kg (60 % coverage). Without adsorbent, the same gas would give 74.8 kPa. With Henry’s law, K = 0.2 mol kg⁻¹ kPa⁻¹, the answer would be 6.8 kPa, because a linear isotherm never saturates.

6. Isosteric heat

At fixed loading q, the Clausius–Clapeyron form applies to the adsorbed phase:

\left(\frac{\partial\ln P}{\partial(1/T)}\right)_q=-\frac{Q_{\text{st}}}{R}

Isotherms at three temperatures give Qst from the slope of ln P against 1/T at each q. In Lab 11’s synthetic data (true Qst = 25 kJ/mol), three isotherms give 25.1 kJ/mol. Recording the 340 K isotherm as 342 K gives 24.1 kJ/mol: a 2 K error moves the heat by 1 kJ/mol.

7. Before Session 4

Write short answers. We use them in the first 30 minutes.

  1. Fusion data. Confirm the two ideal solubilities of naphthalene at 298.15 K in Section 2. Which set would you use, and why?
  2. γ from data. In benzene, m = 4.5226 mol/kg saturates at 294.5 K (M = 78.112 g/mol). Find x₁, then γ₁ with each fusion data set. Is the deviation from ideal positive or negative?
  3. Vessel. In the Section 5 example, why does Henry’s law give a pressure less than half the Langmuir answer, although the two isotherms agree at low P?
