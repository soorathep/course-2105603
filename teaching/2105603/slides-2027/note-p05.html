Mixtures: partial molar properties and the fugacity of a component

Pre-class note for Session P5 · about 20 minutes

Soorathep Kheawhom, Ph.D., FRSC · 2105603 Advanced Chemical Engineering Thermodynamics

Draft for 2027. If you are taking the course this year, use the current course page.

Why read this. Sessions P1–P4 built the thermodynamics of pure fluids. In a mixture each property depends on composition, and the equilibrium condition becomes equal component fugacity in every phase. This note introduces the tools for that step: partial molar properties, the Gibbs–Duhem equation, the component fugacity coefficient and the ideal solution. Work the three questions at the end and bring one question of your own.

1. Partial molar properties

For an extensive property nM of a mixture at fixed T and P, the partial molar property of component i is

\bar M_i=\Big(\frac{\partial(nM)}{\partial n_i}\Big)_{T,P,n_{j\neq i}}.

Because nM is homogeneous of degree one in the amounts (Euler’s theorem), M = Σ xiM̄i. For a binary, the partial molar properties are the intercepts of the tangent to M(x1):

\bar M_1=M+x_2\frac{dM}{dx_1},\qquad\bar M_2=M-x_1\frac{dM}{dx_1}.

The most important one is Ḡi = μi. A partial molar property is a derivative, not the property of one molecule: V̄ of MgSO₄ in very dilute water is negative.

2. The Gibbs–Duhem equation

Differentiating M = Σ xiM̄i and comparing with dM at fixed T and P gives

\sum_ix_i\,d\bar M_i=0\qquad(T,P\ \text{fixed}).

The partial molar properties of a binary are therefore not independent: if one increases with x1, the other decreases. For M = G this is the relation used in Session 2 after the midterm to test measured activity coefficients.

Worked example. Ethanol (1) + water (2) at 298.15 K. Densities measured by Pires et al. (2007) give an excess volume VE = V − x1V1* − x2V2* that is negative everywhere, with a minimum near −1.1 cm³/mol. A three-term Redlich–Kister fit, VE = x1x2[A0 + A1(x1 − x2) + A2(x1 − x2)²], gives A = −4.313, 1.136 and −1.274 cm³/mol. At x1 = 0.5, V̄1 = 57.88 and V̄2 = 16.71 cm³/mol, against 58.68 and 18.07 for the pure liquids. Mixing 50 mL of each gives 96.6 mL.

3. The fugacity of a component

Extending the definition of Session P4 to component i of a mixture at fixed T:

d\mu_i=RT\,d\ln\hat f_i,\qquad\hat\varphi_i=\frac{\hat f_i}{y_iP},\qquad\ln\hat\varphi_i=\int_0^P\big(\bar Z_i-1\big)\frac{dP}{P}.

ln φ̂i is the partial molar property of GR/RT. Equilibrium between phases α and β of a mixture requires f̂iα = f̂iβ for every component.

For a binary gas described by the truncated virial equation, Bmix = y1²B11 + 2y1y2B12 + y2²B22, and

\ln\hat\varphi_1=\frac{P}{RT}\big(B_{11}+y_2^2\delta_{12}\big),\qquad\delta_{12}=2B_{12}-B_{11}-B_{22}.

The cross coefficient B12 describes the interaction of one molecule of 1 with one of 2. It can be measured, or estimated with a combining rule.

4. The ideal solution and the Lewis–Randall rule

In an ideal solution, f̂i = xifi, where fi is the fugacity of pure i at the same T and P (the Lewis–Randall rule). It follows that ΔVmix = 0, ΔHmix = 0, ΔSmix = −RΣxi ln xi and ΔGmix = RTΣxi ln xi. For a virial gas the rule is equivalent to δ12 = 0. It is exact as xi → 1 and fails for a dilute component whose unlike interactions differ from its like ones. The activity coefficient of Session P6 measures that failure in a liquid.

5. Mixing rules for cubic equations

A cubic equation of state describes a mixture with the van der Waals one-fluid rules, a = ΣΣyiyj√(aiaj)(1 − kij) and b = Σyibi. The binary parameter kij is fitted to mixture data. The same equation then gives φ̂i of every phase, the basis of the φ–φ method in Session 1 after the midterm.

6. Before Session P5

Write short answers. We use them in the first 30 minutes.

  1. Tangent intercept. Show that, with the Redlich–Kister form above, dVE/dx1 = A1/2 at x1 = 0.5. Use it to check the two partial molar volumes in Section 2.
  2. Gibbs–Duhem. If V̄1 of ethanol increases by 0.3 cm³/mol between x1 = 0.30 and 0.31, by roughly how much does V̄2 change?
  3. Lewis–Randall. For CO₂ (1) + methane (2) at 298.15 K, B11 = −122.9, B22 = −42.8 and the measured B12 = −64.8 cm³/mol. Compute δ12. Will the Lewis–Randall rule give too high or too low a fugacity for dilute CO₂?
