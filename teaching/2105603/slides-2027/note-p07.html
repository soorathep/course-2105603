3. Choosing a property model

Start from the fluid, the phase and the pressure (Carlson, 1996):

  • Gas at low density (below about half of ρc): the virial equation with B(T). Beyond that, it fails: nitrogen at 300 K and 200 bar has Z = 1.057, and the two-term virial gives 0.957.
  • A pure fluid with a reference equation of state: use it. The NIST values in this course come from such equations.
  • Hydrocarbons and light gases at high pressure, either phase: a cubic EOS with kij. Check the liquid volume separately; a volume shift corrects it at one state without changing Psat (Péneloux et al., 1982).
  • Polar or associating liquids at low pressure: the γ–φ approach with Wilson, NRTL or UNIQUAC and a Psat correlation. Check γ∞ and stability, not only the fit.
  • A possible liquid split: NRTL or UNIQUAC, never Wilson, and LLE data.
  • A dilute solute, or a gas above its Tc: Henry’s law, with a measured Hi or γ∞.
  • No mixture data: UNIFAC or a predictive EOS, as a first estimate before a measurement.

4. The evidence ladder

From weakest to strongest:

  1. Calibration: the model reproduces the data it was fitted to, such as B fitted up to 1 mol/L, or NRTL fitted to GE.
  2. Internal consistency: Gibbs–Duhem on measured γ, a balance that closes, the same H from two paths.
  3. Held-out data: the same kind of measurement at conditions the fit did not use, such as 80 kPa VLE from a 60 kPa fit.
  4. Independent property: a different quantity the model also predicts, such as the liquid volume from an EOS whose ω was fitted to Psat.
  5. Different method: a measured B₁₂ against a combining rule; a directly measured γ∞ against a VLE fit.

A claim is only as strong as the highest rung it reached. An extrapolation, such as γ₁∞ beyond the most dilute measured point, reaches none. Some claims cannot be decided with the data in hand; saying so is part of the answer.

5. Checking code you did not write line by line

From Session P2 you have used AI assistants to write code for step E of each lab. The checks that catch real errors are:

  • a quantity recomputed independently (a Z, a Psat, a γ at one point);
  • a limit the answer must satisfy (Z → 1 as P → 0, γi → 1 as xi → 1, equal fugacity at Psat);
  • the objective re-evaluated at the reported parameters;
  • the lab’s reference values reproduced.

Convergence and a small residual check the solver, not the thermodynamics.
